I build reactors that run on electricity instead of flames. My work uses microwaves and pulsed Joule heating to drive catalytic reactions that furnaces heat slowly and evenly, and it asks what changes when energy reaches the catalyst directly. I measure how materials absorb electromagnetic fields, translate that into reactor design, and carry the result from bench experiments through pilot operation and techno-economic analysis.

About

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Chemical and Biomolecular Engineering at the University of Delaware, advised by Prof. Dionisios G. Vlachos. I study how electromagnetic fields and rapid electrical heating change what a catalyst does, and how that understanding turns into reactors worth building.

Before Delaware I spent four years developing hydrogen and low-carbon fuel processes in Korea, first scaling a sustainable aviation fuel pilot plant at the Institute for Advanced Engineering and then leading liquid organic hydrogen carrier and ammonia decomposition work at the Korea Institute of Science and Technology. That period produced four issued patents and a reactor that releases hydrogen at 2.3 Nm3 per hour.

I expect to complete my Ph.D. in August 2026, and I am looking for research and process development roles in industry and national laboratories.

Research

i.

Microwave-driven catalysis and field–material interactions

A catalyst that absorbs microwaves heats from the inside, and the temperature the reaction sees is not the one a thermocouple reports. I measure dielectric response while the reaction runs so that the chemistry can be attributed to the field rather than assumed from it.

  • Propane dehydrogenation without H2 co-feed, published in Science Advances
  • Dielectric loss scales linearly with oxygen-vacancy concentration in reduced rutile TiO2
  • Collaborations with ITACA in València and INMA in Zaragoza
  • Review under review at Chemical Society Reviews

ii.

Pulsed Joule heating and internally heated reactors

Heating a catalyst in millisecond pulses separates the temperature that makes a product from the temperature that destroys it. Coke and aromatics form on the slow timescale, so the pulse can be tuned to outrun them.

  • Tandem methane coupling and CO2 reduction, published in ACS Energy Letters
  • Short contact time internally heated ethane cracker with reduced capital and CO2 intensity
  • Steam methane reforming and water-gas shift intensified in tandem
  • Experiments paired with microkinetic modeling and CFD

iii.

Hydrogen storage, release, and reactor scale-up

Liquid organic hydrogen carriers and ammonia only work as fuels if the release step is efficient at scale. The limit is usually heat delivery and platinum loading rather than catalyst activity.

  • Atomic layer deposition on abundant oxide supports, published in Angewandte Chemie
  • Dehydrogenation reactor scaled to 2.3 Nm3 h-1 using phase-change thermal management
  • Ni-alloy foam architectures for Joule-heated ammonia decomposition
  • Funded by Hyundai Motor Company and the National Research Foundation of Korea

iv.

Process development for low-carbon fuels and chemicals

A result at the bench is only useful if the flowsheet around it closes. I work on the scale-up side as well, from pilot operation and safety procedure to process models that set design guidance.

  • 5 ton per year sustainable aviation fuel pilot meeting ASTM D7566 specifications
  • Hydrogen plant flowsheet modeling and carbon intensity guidance at Linde Engineering
  • Equilibrium-kinetic model for high-temperature water-gas shift performance
  • Energy and economic analyses of hydrogen production routes
